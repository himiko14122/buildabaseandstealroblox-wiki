Layered defense is the foundational principle of serious base building in Build a Base and Steal. A single layer of walls and traps is a speed bump. Multiple layers of walls, traps, and obstacles are a real defense, because each layer costs the raider time, resources, and momentum. The raider who breaches the first layer is not at the loot. The raider who breaches the second layer is still not at the loot. By the time the raider has breached enough layers to reach the valuables, they have used most of their raid time and most of their gear, and the extraction window has shrunk. This guide explains why layering works, how to design effective layers, and how to balance cost against protection.

## Why Layered Defense Works

The core principle of layered defense is that each layer imposes a cost on the attacker, and the cumulative cost of breaching all the layers exceeds the value of the loot. A single wall costs the raider a few seconds and one breaching charge. Two walls cost double. Three walls cost triple. The total cost scales with the number of layers, and the value of the loot does not change, so the math eventually favors the defender.

The second reason layering works is psychological. A raider who sees multiple layers knows the raid will be longer, harder, and more expensive than a raid on a single-layer base. Many raiders will skip a layered base and look for an easier target. The psychological deterrent is often more effective than the actual defensive strength, because the raider never even attempts the raid.

The third reason is the compounding effect of traps. Traps placed in layers are more effective than traps placed randomly, because the raider who has already taken damage from the first trap is more cautious and moves more slowly, which makes them more likely to trigger the second trap. The traps reinforce each other when they are placed in layers.

### The Three Standard Layers

A typical serious base in Build a Base and Steal has three layers: the outer perimeter, the middle buffer, and the inner vault. Each layer serves a different purpose, and the layers are designed to be progressively harder to breach.

The outer perimeter is the first line of defense. It consists of basic walls, low-tier traps, and minimal obstacles. The purpose of the outer perimeter is not to stop a determined raider. It is to slow down casual raiders, signal the strength of the base, and force any raider to expend basic resources like breaching charges. The outer perimeter is the cheapest layer, and it is the one that gets replaced most often after raids.

The middle buffer is the second line of defense. It consists of upgraded walls, mid-tier traps, and some obstacles. The purpose of the middle buffer is to impose real costs on raiders who have breached the outer perimeter. By the time a raider reaches the middle buffer, they have already spent basic resources, and the middle buffer forces them to spend more. The middle buffer is more expensive than the outer perimeter, and it is replaced less often.

The inner vault is the third line of defense. It consists of the highest-tier walls, the most expensive traps, and a small controlled entrance. The purpose of the inner vault is to protect the most valuable pets and the most critical resources. The inner vault is the most expensive layer, and it is designed to be replaced only when the raider has spent so much time and resources that the raid timer has run out.

### Layer Composition

Each layer should be composed of a mix of walls, traps, and obstacles, not just one type of defense. A layer of only walls is easy to breach with a breaching charge, because the charge goes through any wall. A layer of only traps is easy to breach with shields, because the shield absorbs the trap damage. A layer of walls, traps, and obstacles forces the raider to deal with all three types, which is much harder.

The walls in each layer should be of the appropriate tier for that layer. The outer perimeter should use the cheapest walls that still provide meaningful protection. The middle buffer should use mid-tier walls that require more breaching effort. The inner vault should use the highest-tier walls that you can afford, because the vault is the section you will not voluntarily replace.

The traps in each layer should be placed strategically, not randomly. The most effective trap placement is near the entrance to the layer, where the raider is most committed and most likely to take damage. Traps should also be placed at choke points, where the raider cannot avoid them by going around. A single well-placed trap is more effective than five poorly placed ones.

The obstacles in each layer should slow down the raider, not block them entirely. A row of small obstacles that the raider must walk around is more effective than a wall that the raider can breach. The obstacles force the raider to spend time, which is the resource you are trying to drain.

### Layer Transitions

The transition between layers is a critical design decision. A smooth transition (where the layers flow into each other) is easy to navigate for the raider. A sharp transition (where the layers are clearly separated) forces the raider to make decisions at the transition point, which costs time and increases the chance of triggering traps.

The best layer transitions include a chokepoint, which is a narrow space that the raider must pass through to enter the next layer. The chokepoint should be lined with traps and have walls on both sides. A raider in a chokepoint cannot go around the traps and cannot breach the walls without using a charge, so the chokepoint is the most effective defensive feature in the entire base.

A second design element is the dead end, which is a path that leads to a wall and forces the raider to turn around. The dead end is most effective when it appears to be the main path to the loot, because the raider commits to the path and then has to backtrack. The dead end is a psychological weapon as much as a physical one, and it costs you almost nothing to create.

## Cost-Benefit Analysis

Each layer has a cost, and the cost of the layer should be proportional to the value of what it protects. The outer perimeter is the cheapest layer, because it protects relatively low-value assets. The middle buffer is more expensive, because it protects mid-value assets. The inner vault is the most expensive, because it protects the highest-value assets.

A common mistake is to spend too much on the outer perimeter and not enough on the inner vault. The result is a base that looks impressive from the outside but is hollow on the inside, and a raider who breaches the outer perimeter finds the loot almost undefended. The fix is to shift budget from the outer perimeter to the inner vault, making the inner vault significantly stronger than the outer perimeter.

A second common mistake is to spend equally on all three layers. The result is a base where each layer is mediocre, and a determined raider can breach all three without much difficulty. The fix is to differentiate the layers, with the inner vault significantly stronger than the middle buffer, which is significantly stronger than the outer perimeter. The differentiation is what makes the layered defense effective.

## Adapting the Layers

The layers should be adapted to the current meta, the current event, and the current threat level. During events that boost raid rewards, the base should be more heavily layered, because more raiders are active. During quiet periods, the base can be less heavily layered, because fewer raiders are present.

The layers should also be adapted to your specific vulnerability. If you are being raided from a particular direction (because the entrance is facing a high-traffic area), reinforce the walls on that direction. If you are being raided by a particular type of raider (like a stealth raider who avoids traps), add more walls and fewer traps. The adaptation is what keeps the defense effective over time.

The final adaptation is the rotation, which is the practice of moving the valuable pets to a different location within the base on a regular basis. The rotation defeats raiders who have studied your base layout and know exactly where to go. Combined with layered defense, rotation is one of the most effective defensive techniques in the game.
