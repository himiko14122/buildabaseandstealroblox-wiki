Walls are the single most important defensive element in Build a Base and Steal. Every other system, traps, turrets, surveillance, and pets themselves, exists because walls created a perimeter worth protecting. A player who understands walls will outlast a player who relies on gadgets, because walls scale with every other defensive investment. This guide explains how walls work, which materials matter, where to place them, and how to design a perimeter that turns casual raids away before they cost you a single pet.

## How Walls Work in Build a Base and Steal

Walls in Build a Base and Steal are destructible structures that block raider movement and channel attackers into predictable paths. They have three core attributes: hit points, material tier, and coverage. Hit points determine how much damage a wall can absorb before breaking. Material tier determines both the hit points and the cost. Coverage determines how much of an opening a single wall piece blocks, measured in tile units on the grid.

The relationship between these three attributes defines your base's defensive profile. A wall with high hit points but poor coverage creates gaps raiders can walk through. A wall with excellent coverage but low hit points breaks in a single hit. The best bases balance all three, which is why a thoughtful layout almost always beats a brute-force wall stack.

### Wall Hit Points Explained

Each material tier has a defined hit point pool. Wooden walls absorb roughly one to two standard gear hits, stone walls absorb three to four, metal walls absorb five to seven, and reinforced alloy walls absorb eight to twelve. The exact numbers shift with balance updates, but the relative ratios stay constant. When a raider is choosing between two bases, hit point totals are the first thing they compare, because hit points determine how long a raid will take and therefore how much it will cost.

Hit points also degrade. A wall that has taken partial damage is not equivalent to a fresh wall. Most players underestimate how much damage accumulates over a season. A wall that absorbed two raids without breaking is functionally weaker than the same wall freshly placed. Periodic inspection and replacement is part of serious base defense.

### Coverage and Tile Geometry

Walls snap to a grid, and each wall piece covers one or more tiles. Standard straight walls cover one tile. Corner walls cover two. Wide walls cover three. Some special materials cover a half-tile to allow finer control. When you plan a base, you should think in tiles, not in wall pieces. A common beginner mistake is to count wall pieces rather than counting coverage, which leaves awkward gaps that raiders exploit.

The grid also determines where walls can be placed. Walls cannot overlap with pets, traps, or other build objects. They cannot be placed outside your plot boundary. They must connect to the ground or to another wall. Understanding these constraints before you build saves a great deal of frustration, because it prevents the late-game scenario where you cannot complete a defensive layer because the grid rejects the placement.

## Wall Material Tier Guide

Choosing the right material for each section of your base is a strategic decision, not a stylistic one. The materials available in Build a Base and Steal, from cheapest to most expensive, are wood, stone, metal, and reinforced alloy. Each tier trades cost for hit points and for secondary properties like fire resistance, explosive resistance, and visual prominence.

### Wood Walls

Wood is the starting material and remains useful throughout the game. Wood walls are cheap, fast to place, and easy to replace after a raid. The trade-off is hit points. A determined raider with mid-tier gear will break wood walls in one or two hits. Use wood in the outermost layer of your base, where you want to slow down raiders without investing real resources, and where you expect to repair frequently.

Wood has one overlooked advantage: it is quiet. When a wooden wall is damaged, it produces a softer sound than stone or metal. Some experienced players use this to their advantage, placing wood in the outermost layer to avoid alerting defenders across the map when raiders test the perimeter. The cost savings of wood in the outer layer are usually worth more than the hit point loss.

### Stone Walls

Stone is the workhorse material for most bases. Stone walls cost three to four times as much as wood and have roughly double the hit points. They are durable enough to absorb a full raid attempt and cheap enough to build an entire base out of in mid-game. Most serious players build their first real wall layer out of stone, and many never switch to higher tiers for outer defenses.

Stone also has better resistance to fire and basic explosives. If raiders use fire-based gear, stone walls survive longer than wood, which is a meaningful difference when the raid includes a flame wave that would otherwise sweep through your outer perimeter in seconds. The slight cost premium of stone over wood pays for itself in durability and resistance.

### Metal Walls

Metal walls are the late-game standard. They cost roughly five times as much as wood and offer three to four times the hit points. Metal also resists advanced explosives and most electrical gear. If a raider shows up with the strongest gear available, metal walls force them to spend their best charges, which often costs them more than the raid is worth. This is the principle of deterrent defense: a metal wall is not invincible, but it makes a raid so expensive that the raider calculates the profit is no longer worth the risk.

Metal walls are also visually intimidating. When a raider scopes a base and sees metal, they assume the player inside is experienced and well-resourced. That assumption alone filters out a large portion of would-be attackers. The psychological value of metal is real, even if the hit point difference from stone is smaller than the visual difference suggests.

### Reinforced Alloy Walls

Reinforced alloy is the top tier. It costs roughly ten times as much as wood and absorbs almost any single hit from the strongest gear in the game. A reinforced alloy wall will survive multiple high-end breaching attempts. The cost is high enough that only the deepest pockets can afford to build an entire base out of it, and most players reserve reinforced alloy for the inner core where the most valuable pets live.

Because reinforced alloy is so expensive, raiders who see it often assume the base contains a legendary or mythical pet. This creates a high-stakes signal: reinforced alloy both deters casual raiders and attracts the most committed ones. If you use reinforced alloy, be prepared for a focused raid on the section you placed it in. Pair it with traps and active defenses to make the section genuinely dangerous.

## Strategic Wall Placement

Material choice matters less than placement. A well-placed stone wall deters more raids than a poorly placed reinforced alloy wall. Placement has three primary concerns: coverage, channeling, and weak point reinforcement.

### Coverage and Perimeter Closure

Your outer wall layer should fully enclose your base with no gaps wider than half a tile. Raiders look for gaps. A half-tile opening might as well be a welcome mat, because the smallest pets can be stolen through it, and raiders use gaps to peek inside and scout your pet arrangement before committing. Walk the perimeter of your base once a week and look for any spot where you can see daylight between two walls. Close it.

Coverage also extends upward. Walls in Build a Base and Steal have a default height, but you can extend them with additional wall pieces stacked vertically. Vertical coverage is rarely a concern in early game, but once raid gear starts including climbing tools and aerial pets, vertical extensions become necessary. A two-tile high wall is more than enough for most bases; three tiles is fortress territory.

### Channeling Raiders into Kill Zones

The most powerful use of walls is not to block raiders but to control where they go. A straight wall with no obstacles is just a delay. A wall that funnels raiders into a narrow corridor covered by traps is a kill zone. Channeling is the art of using wall geometry to remove the raider's options and force them into your prepared defenses.

Channeling is achieved through three patterns. The first is the funnel, where two angled walls narrow an entrance from wide to narrow. The second is the maze, where walls create winding paths that slow raiders and trigger multiple trap layers. The third is the cul-de-sac, where a wall section creates a dead end that traps raiders inside your trap network. Each pattern serves a different defensive philosophy, and most experienced bases combine all three in different sections.

### Weak Point Reinforcement

Every base has weak points. The most common are corners, the entrance, and the section closest to the highest-value pet. Reinforcing weak points means using stronger materials in those specific locations rather than spreading your resources evenly. A wall made entirely of stone is weaker than a wall made of wood at the entrance and reinforced alloy at the core, because concentrated defense is more effective than uniform defense.

The entrance is the single most important weak point. It is the only part of your base that raiders must cross to reach your pets, and it is the only part that you use regularly yourself. If the entrance wall is weak, raiders can breach it in seconds. If the entrance wall is strong and trap-covered, every raid takes longer and costs more. Invest in your entrance first, your core second, and your perimeter third.

## Common Wall Mistakes to Avoid

The most expensive wall mistakes are not the ones that cost you cash. They are the ones that cost you pets. Building walls without testing them, building walls in patterns raiders can predict, and building walls without maintaining them are the three failures that separate successful bases from raided ones.

### Predictable Layouts

Raiders who have played Build a Base and Steal for any length of time recognize common base shapes. The perfect rectangle, the concentric circles, the four-quadrant grid — these shapes are easy to read because they appear in every base guide. If your base is a textbook shape, raiders know where to enter, where the traps likely sit, and where the valuables are stored. Break the pattern. Add a wing, an extension, an L-shape, a T-junction. Anything that makes the base look slightly unusual makes it harder to raid.

### Neglected Maintenance

Walls decay slowly over time, even without raids. They also accumulate damage from failed raids that you may not have noticed. Walking the perimeter of your base every few days and inspecting every wall is a habit that prevents catastrophic failures. A wall with 20% hit points is a wall that will break on the next serious raid, and you may not be online when that raid happens.

### Outer Layer Overinvestment

The outermost wall layer takes the most damage. Investing too much in the outer layer means you are spending premium materials on a section that will need replacement regularly. Reserve premium materials for the core and use cheaper materials for the outer layer, accepting that the outer layer is expendable. This is a hard psychological adjustment for new players, because it feels wrong to deliberately build a weak outer layer, but it is the foundation of sustainable defense economics.

## Advanced Wall Techniques

Once you have mastered basic placement, three advanced techniques can take your base to the next level: layered walls, decoy walls, and breakaway walls.

### Layered Walls

Layered walls are two parallel walls with a small gap between them. The outer wall takes the first hits, and the inner wall provides backup if the outer wall falls. Layered walls cost more, but they are dramatically harder to breach because raiders must break through both layers. A common configuration is wood on the outside and stone on the inside, with traps in the gap. The wood absorbs the first wave, the traps damage the raider, and the stone stops anything that gets through.

### Decoy Walls

Decoy walls are weak walls placed to look like the main defense. They channel raiders into a section of the base where the real pets are not stored. Decoy walls require a second, hidden area of the base where the actual valuables are kept, separated from the decoy by a hidden door or a wall section that is easy for you to bypass but hard for raiders to find. Decoy walls work because most raiders do not have time to fully search a base during a raid. They grab the obvious loot and leave. The obvious loot is the decoy.

### Breakaway Walls

Breakaway walls are weak sections deliberately placed to break first under pressure. The breakaway is positioned so that when it falls, raiders flood into a kill zone rather than reaching the core. This is the opposite of reinforcement: you deliberately weaken a wall to control where the breach happens. Breakaway walls are an advanced technique because they require careful trap placement on the other side, but they are devastatingly effective when used correctly.

## Measuring Wall Effectiveness

How do you know if your walls are working? The answer is in the raid logs. Build a Base and Steal tracks every raid attempt, including how long the raid lasted, where the breach occurred, and what was stolen. Review these logs weekly. If a particular wall section is breached in every raid, reinforce it. If a section is never breached, consider whether the cost of maintaining it is justified, or whether you can downgrade that section to a cheaper material and reallocate the savings.

The logs also reveal which materials are performing best. If stone walls are lasting longer than metal in your server's current meta, that is information you can use. Materials are not equally effective in every server; the gear available, the player skill level, and the active balance patch all influence which material is optimal. The logs tell you which material is winning in your specific environment, and that is the material you should prioritize.

## Conclusion: Walls Are Strategy, Not Decoration

Walls in Build a Base and Steal are the foundation of every successful defense. They are also the most customizable element, with four material tiers, three advanced techniques, and a placement grid that rewards thoughtful design. New players should start with stone walls in a closed perimeter and an obvious entrance. Experienced players should layer walls, use decoys, reinforce weak points, and review their raid logs to refine the design over time. The base that survives the longest is rarely the base with the most expensive walls; it is the base with the best-placed walls, the most appropriate materials, and the most consistent maintenance. Master walls and the rest of defense follows.
