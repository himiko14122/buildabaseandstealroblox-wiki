Every Build a Base and Steal player gets raided eventually, and how you respond to a successful raid determines whether the experience makes your base stronger or starts a downward spiral. Players who panic after a raid make emotional decisions that often make the base worse. Players who treat the raid as a learning opportunity make deliberate changes that strengthen the base. This guide covers the post-raid process, from the initial damage assessment through the long-term improvements that turn a defeated base into a hardened fortress.

## The First Hour After the Raid

The first hour after a successful raid is the most emotionally charged, and the decisions you make during this hour are often the worst. The natural impulse is to immediately rebuild everything that was destroyed, to spend the cash reserve on replacing the lost pets, and to add more walls in a panic. These impulses are understandable, but they usually lead to suboptimal outcomes.

The right approach in the first hour is to do nothing structural. Take a walk around the base, look at the damage, and form an initial assessment. The damage tells you what the raider was able to breach and what they were not. The breaches are the most important data, because they identify the weak points in your defense.

The first hour is also the right time to check the raid log, which shows exactly what was stolen and what was destroyed. The log is the authoritative source for the damage, and it is more accurate than your memory of the base. The log also shows the time of the raid, which can help you identify patterns (like the raider always attacks at a specific time of day).

### The Damage Assessment

The damage assessment is the structured review of the raid, and it should be done in the 1 to 24 hours after the raid. The assessment has three parts: the breach path, the loot taken, and the trap performance.

The breach path is the route the raider took through your base, from the entrance to the loot. The breach path tells you which walls failed and in what order, which traps were triggered and which were not, and whether the raider followed your intended defensive layout or found a way around it. The breach path is the most important data point, because it tells you what the raider exploited.

The loot taken is the list of pets and resources that were extracted. The loot taken tells you what the raider considered valuable, which is usually a good proxy for what you should consider valuable. If the raider took your legendary pets, they were after the highest-value targets. If they took your common pets, they were after quick cash. The loot taken should inform your future pet placement strategy.

The trap performance is the assessment of how each trap performed. The trap performance tells you which traps were effective and which were not. A trap that was triggered but did not stop the raider is a candidate for upgrade. A trap that was not triggered is either poorly placed or not in the breach path. The trap performance should inform your future trap placement.

### Prioritizing Repairs

Once you have completed the damage assessment, the next step is to prioritize the repairs. The repairs should be prioritized based on their impact on the defense, not on the cost or the ease of the repair.

The first priority is to repair the breach points. The breach points are the weakest parts of your defense, and leaving them unrepaired invites a second raid. The breach repair should use the same tier of wall or trap that was destroyed, or a higher tier if your budget allows. A higher tier at the breach point makes that point stronger than it was before the raid.

The second priority is to reinforce the entry path. The entry path is the route the raider took to reach the breach point, and reinforcing it makes the next raid harder. The reinforcement can include additional walls, additional traps, or obstacles that slow down the raider. The reinforcement should be informed by the breach path analysis.

The third priority is to replace the lost pets and resources. The replacement is important, but it should not come at the expense of the defense. A base with a strong defense and a smaller pet collection is more secure than a base with a weak defense and a full pet collection. The replacement should be done within the budget allocated for it, and the budget should be smaller than the defensive repair budget.

### The Learning Mindset

The most important aspect of post-raid recovery is the learning mindset, which treats the raid as a free lesson in base defense. The raider who successfully breached your base has demonstrated a specific attack pattern, and the pattern reveals a specific weakness in your defense. The fix is to address the weakness, not just the symptoms.

The learning mindset also treats the raid as a data point. A single raid is not enough to identify all the weaknesses, but it is enough to identify one or two. Over multiple raids, the data accumulates, and the patterns become clearer. A player who has been raided ten times has ten data points, and the analysis of those ten data points will reveal the most important weaknesses to address.

The learning mindset is the opposite of the victim mindset. The victim mindset says "I got unlucky" and changes nothing. The learning mindset says "I got tested" and changes everything. The two mindsets produce very different outcomes, and the learning mindset is the foundation of long-term base defense.

### Long-Term Improvements

The post-raid period is also a good time to make long-term improvements that go beyond the immediate repairs. The long-term improvements are the changes that address the structural weaknesses that allowed the raid to succeed, and they are the most impactful changes you can make.

A long-term improvement example is changing the entrance location. If the raider breached the entrance easily, the entrance may be in a poor location, and moving it to a different part of the base may make the next raid harder. The cost of moving the entrance is moderate, and the benefit is significant.

Another long-term improvement example is changing the pet placement. If the raider extracted your valuable pets quickly, the pet placement may have been too predictable, and spreading the pets across multiple locations may slow down the next extraction. The cost of changing the placement is low, and the benefit is high.

A third long-term improvement example is adding a new trap type. If the raider was able to avoid your existing traps, the existing trap types may not be effective against the current raider meta, and adding a new trap type may catch the raider off guard. The cost of adding a trap is moderate, and the benefit is the diversification of your defensive capabilities.

## The Repelling Effect

A base that has been successfully raided once is more likely to be raided again, because raiders share information about successful targets. The repelling effect means that the post-raid period is also the time when your base is most vulnerable, and the response must be fast enough to prevent a second raid.

The fastest response is to immediately repair the breach points and add at least one new defensive element. The new defensive element can be a trap, an obstacle, or a wall, and its purpose is to signal to potential raiders that the base is being actively defended. A base that is being actively defended is less attractive than a base that appears abandoned, and the new defensive element is the signal.

The response should also include logging in more frequently for the next few days. Frequent logins allow you to respond to raids in progress, which deters raiders who do not want to face active defense. The frequent logins are a temporary measure, and they can be reduced once the new defenses are in place and the base is back to a defensible state.

## Building Back Stronger

The best post-raid strategy is to build the base back stronger than it was before the raid. The strategy accepts that the raid happened and uses it as motivation to make improvements that were not made before. The improvements make the base more resilient to future raids, and the experience of recovering from a raid makes the player more disciplined about defense.

The build-back-stronger approach also has a psychological benefit. A player who has recovered from a raid and improved the base has demonstrated that they can handle setbacks, which builds confidence. The confidence translates into better decisions in the future, and the cycle of improvement continues.

The build-back-stronger approach is the opposite of the rebuild-from-scratch approach, which abandons the base and starts over. The rebuild-from-scratch approach is occasionally justified, but it is usually a sign that the player has not learned from the raid. The base that was raided once can be hardened against future raids, and the player who hardens it has invested in the long-term defensive capability of their account.
