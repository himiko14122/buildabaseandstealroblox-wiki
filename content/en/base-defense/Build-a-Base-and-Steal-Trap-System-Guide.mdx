Traps are the active layer of defense in Build a Base and Steal. Where walls slow raiders and channel them, traps stop them. A well-placed trap network can defeat a raid that would otherwise strip your base, and a poorly-placed trap is a waste of resources that could have funded a wall upgrade. This guide covers every trap type, the placement principles that turn individual traps into networks, and the chain patterns that experienced players use to lock down high-value sections of their bases.

## Trap Categories in Build a Base and Steal

Traps fall into four functional categories: damage traps, control traps, alarm traps, and utility traps. Each category serves a different role in a defensive network, and the most effective bases use all four. Understanding the category of each trap is the first step toward designing a system rather than scattering individual traps randomly around your plot.

### Damage Traps

Damage traps deal hit point loss to raiders and their gear. The most common damage trap is the spike trap, which activates when a raider steps on a pressure plate and deals moderate damage over a fire trap, which deals damage over time and can spread to adjacent areas, making it effective against grouped raiders. The fourth is the electric trap, which deals heavy damage to gear and shields but only light damage to the raider.

Damage traps are the foundation of any trap network. Without them, a trap system is just a delay tactic. With them, every step the raider takes into your base costs them resources. The most effective damage trap placement is at the end of a channeling path, where the raider is committed to moving forward and cannot easily retreat. A raider who has taken a spike trap hit and an electric trap hit before reaching your core is a raider who is making economic decisions about whether to continue.

### Control Traps

Control traps do not deal damage. They slow, immobilize, or disorient raiders. The slow trap reduces movement speed for several seconds. The freeze trap immobilizes the raider for a brief window. The blind trap reduces accuracy and the ability to see traps beyond a small radius. The web trap roots the raider in place until they break free with a gear charge or a teammate's help.

Control traps are most effective when combined with damage and hit points, which compounds the cost of the raid. The classic combination is a slow trap leading into a spike trap, which holds the raider in the damage area for longer. Experienced players chain control and damage in alternating layers so that every step the raider takes triggers both effects.

### Alarm Traps

Alarm traps do no damage and exert no control. They simply notify you that a raider is in a specific part of your base. The motion sensor is the simplest alarm, triggering when a raider crosses its detection radius. The pressure plate is more specific, triggering only when stepped on. The audio sensor triggers on the sound of a wall being damaged, which can alert you to a breach attempt before the wall actually falls.

Alarm traps are valuable for two reasons. First, they tell you which section of your base is under attack, so you can respond appropriately. Second, they create psychological pressure. A raider who knows alarms are watching them often raids less efficiently, because they are spending mental energy on detection avoidance rather than on finding your pets. Many experienced players place visible alarm traps near the entrance as a deterrent, even when the rest of the base has few alarms.

### Utility Traps

Utility traps provide indirect defensive value. The is the repair trap, which repairs nearby walls when triggered, useful for bases that take frequent low-level raids. The third is the visibility trap, which highlights any raider inside its radius to all defenders in the area, making them easier to target.

Utility traps are situational. They shine in specific base designs and add little value in others. A repair trap is useless in a base with no walls to repair. A visibility trap is critical in a base designed for active defense but worthless in a base designed to be self-sufficient while you are offline. Choose utility traps that match your overall defensive philosophy.

## Trap Placement Principles

A trap's value depends almost entirely on where it is placed. The same spike trap that is devastating in a corridor is irrelevant in an open room. The principles below describe how experienced players think about placement, and they apply to every trap category.

### Place Traps Where Raiders Must Go

The first rule of trap placement is to place traps in paths raiders are forced to walk. Doorways, narrow corridors, and the approach to the core-value locations, because raiders can simply avoid them. A trap that the raider can walk around is not a trap; it is decoration.

The most powerful placement locations are the points where channeling walls converge. When two angled walls narrow an entrance from three tiles to one, the single tile at the narrow point is the highest-value trap location in the base. Place your most expensive trap there. Raiders will cross that tile no matter what, because there is no other path. Every raid pays the cost of that trap.

### Layer Traps in Depth

Single traps are easy to deal with. A raider who sees one spike trap steps around it, or triggers it and moves on. Layered traps are harder. Place one trap immediately after another in the same path, so the raider who avoids the first walks directly into the second. The most effective layering places control traps before damage traps, so the raider is slowed or rooted when the damage trap triggers.

Depth layering extends the principle across multiple sections. Place one across three layers is significantly more than the cost of any single layer. Most experienced players aim for at least three trap layers in any high-value base.

### Mix Trap Types in the Same Layer

A layer made of three spike traps is weaker than a layer made of one spike trap, one slow trap, and one electric trap. The reason is predictability. A raider who recognizes a spike trap knows what to expect and can respond. A raider who triggers a slow trap and then walks into a spike trap and then into an electric trap is responding to three different situations in rapid succession, and most raiders cannot do that effectively. Mix trap types within each layer to force the raider to switch tactics.

The mix should also include a control effect. A damage trap without a control trap can be avoided by skilled raiders who see it coming. A control trap without a damage trap is a delay without consequence. Combine the two, and every step the raider takes costs them both time and hit points. The combination is what makes a trap network dangerous rather than annoying.

### Hide Traps in Plain Sight

Visible traps are still useful, because they slow cautious raiders and force them to spend gear charges on detection. Hidden traps are more of decoy loot, a wall they want to breach, or a pet they want to steal. Place the trap slightly behind or beneath the obvious target, so the raider is focused on the target and walks into the trap.

Hidden does not mean invisible. Build a Base and Steal traps are visible to raiders who are looking carefully, especially with detection gear. The point is not to make the trap impossible to see but to make it easy to overlook. A trap placed directly under a decoy chest is hard to see because the raider's attention is on the chest. A trap placed in an obvious empty hallway is easy to see because there is nothing else to look at.

## Trap Chains and Cascade Designs

The most advanced trap systems are chains, where triggering one trap activates another. Chains create compound effects that single traps cannot match. A classic chain is a pressure plate connected to a slow trap, which is positioned so that the slow effect holds the raider inside an explosive trap's radius. The raider steps on the plate, slows down, and then takes the explosive. Three traps, one action, devastating result.

### The Entry Chain

The entry chain sits just inside the entrance and is designed to punish the slowed, then damaged, then frozen, all within the first three steps. The chain should be short enough that the raider cannot back out after the first trigger, but long enough to cover multiple trap effects. Two tiles of depth is usually ideal.

### The Channeling Chain

The channeling chain sits in a narrow corridor between two walls. The walls prevent the raider from going around the chain, and the corridor forces them through every trap in sequence. The most effective channeling chains use three to five traps, alternating control and damage, and ending with a high-cooldown trap like an explosive that the raider cannot wait out. The chain should be expensive to build but devastating in effect.

### The Core Chain

The core chain sits around the highest-value pet or resource. The core chain is the last line of defense, so it should be the most expensive and the most reliable. The most common core chain is a freeze trap, a repair trap, and an alarm trap. The freeze stops the raider long enough for the repair to reinforce nearby walls and the alarm to notify you. The chain is defensive rather than offensive, because at the core, your goal is to slow the raider down until you can respond and cost cash to repair. The economics of trap networks are different from walls because traps have ongoing maintenance costs. A trap that triggers during a raid is partially depleted, and depleted traps deal reduced damage or shorter control. Maintaining a trap network means spending a portion of your income on trap upkeep, which is a real cost that should be factored into your overall defense budget.

### Sizing Your Trap Budget

A common mistake is to over-invest in traps relative to walls. The recommended split for a balanced base is 40% walls, 30% traps, 20% pets and storage, and 10% reserve. The exact split depends on your playstyle, but if you find yourself constantly low on cash for walls, your trap network is too expensive. Trim traps before you trim walls, because walls are the foundation that traps depend on.

### Trap Tiers and When to Upgrade

Traps come in three tiers, similar to walls. Tier 1 traps are cheap and weak. Tier 2 traps are mid-cost and reliable. Tier 3 traps are expensive and powerful. The trap tier you should use depends on the value of the section you if you are protecting legendary or mythical pets.

## Common Trap Mistakes

The most common trap mistakes are predictable. Avoiding them is the difference between a defensive network and a waste of resources.

### Trapping the Wrong Path

Placing a strong trap in a path raiders do not use is the most common error. The path raiders use is not always the path you think they will use. After every raid, look at where the breach occurred and where the raider moved inside your base. The path they took is the path you should trap most heavily. If you trap a different path, you are optimizing for a hypothetical that does not match your actual threat.

### Overlapping Trap Effects

Placing two slow traps in the same location does not double the slow effect. It wastes one trap slot and gives the raider no additional disadvantage. Each trap should contribute a unique effect. A slow trap and a damage trap in the same spot is good. Two slow traps in the same spot is wasteful. Review your trap layout and check for redundant effects. Replace redundant traps with different types or move them to a different path.

### Trapping the Entrance Only

The entrance is the obvious trap location, but it is also the easiest to detect and avoid. Skilled raiders approach are the ones inside the base, where detection gear is less effective and where the channeling walls force them into predictable paths. Place fewer traps at the entrance and more traps in the middle of the base.

## Measuring Trap Effectiveness

The way to know if your traps are working is in the raid outcomes. If a raid ends with the raider taking significant damage and your pets intact, your traps are doing their job. If a raid ends with the raider walking out with your best pet and your traps at full charge, your traps are misplaced or too weak. Review the raid log and check the trap trigger history. A trap that has not triggered in months is either hidden too well or placed in a path raiders do not use.

The most important metric is the average cost the raider pays per raid, measured in gear charges, health, and time. A good trap network makes every raid cost the raider more than the raid is worth. If you can achieve that, your base will be raided less often over time, because word spreads in the community about which bases are too expensive to attack.

## Conclusion: Traps Are Networks, Not Decorations

Traps in Build a Base and Steal are not individual objects. They are nodes in a network. The network has layers, against skilled raiders. A player who designs trap networks will be designing the conditions under which raids fail. Start with a three-layer trap system, alternating control and damage, and refine based on your raid logs. The most successful trap networks are not the most expensive; they are the most thoughtfully placed.
