Pet storage is one of the first bottlenecks every Build a Base and Steal player hits, and how you manage it determines whether your collection grows efficiently or stalls out within the first week. The default storage cap is small enough to fill up after a few hours of active rolling, and beginners who do not plan for the cap find themselves unable to roll for new pets because there is no room to store them. This guide covers the storage mechanics, the expansion options, the organizational system that experienced players use, and the discipline that keeps a collection healthy as it grows into the hundreds.

## How Storage Works in Build a Base and Steal

Every pet you roll takes up one storage slot, and the storage has a hard cap that is shared across your account. The default cap starts at 30 slots, which is enough for the first day or two of play but quickly becomes the binding constraint on growth. When your storage is full, the rolling machine will still spin, but the resulting pet will either be sent to a temporary inbox or auto-recycled into a small cash refund, depending on the rarity. Neither outcome is what you want, because the inbox has a much smaller cap than the main storage, and the auto-recycle prevents you from collecting valuable drops.

The storage cap is per account, not per plot, which means that even if you abandon your current base and start a new one, your storage follows you. The cap also applies to all rarities, so a storage filled with 30 common pets cannot accept a new legendary until you make room. This is the most common reason new players lose valuable drops: they were saving commons "just in case" and missed the legendary that rolled in during a hot streak. The fix is to treat storage as a dynamic resource that you manage actively, not as a passive inventory that you fill up indefinitely.

### The Default Cap and Expansion Options

The default cap of 30 slots can be expanded in three ways. The first is the cash upgrade, which is available from the storage menu and increases the cap by 10 or 20 slots per purchase, with each purchase costing more than the last. The second is the Premium Pass, which provides a permanent 50-slot boost to all players who purchase it, plus an additional 20-slot boost for the highest tier. The third is the event reward, where some events grant storage expansion tokens as milestone rewards.

The cash upgrade is the most accessible and should be prioritized by free-to-play players. The first expansion (from 30 to 50) is the most cost-effective, and it gives you enough headroom to roll actively for several days before you need to think about storage again. The second expansion (from 50 to 80) is moderately priced and is worth doing once you have a stable income. The third expansion (from 80 to 120) is expensive and is usually reserved for players who are actively chasing high-tier collection bonuses. Beyond 120, the prices become steep enough that the upgrade is mostly relevant to veteran players with established pet collections.

### What to Keep and What to Recycle

The hardest part of pet storage management is deciding which pets to keep and which to recycle. The general rule is to keep pets that contribute to family bonuses, to keep pets that are rare or higher, and to recycle the rest. The general rule has nuances, and the right answer depends on your current collection priorities.

Common pets are the most frequent candidates for recycling. A single common pet is worth almost nothing in the trading market, and the cash refund from recycling is usually 20 to 50 percent of the roll cost, which means that a common pet sitting in storage is a constant small drag on your rolling budget. The exception is common pets that complete a family bonus. If you have three of four pets in a family and the fourth is a common, keeping the common is the right call, because the family bonus is worth more than the recycle value.

Uncommon pets are worth keeping in most cases, because their income rate is meaningfully higher than commons and they contribute to uncommon family bonuses. The exception is duplicate uncommons that you already have multiple copies of, because the marginal value of a fifth uncommon in a family is much lower than the marginal value of the first one. If you have two copies of a common uncommon family, a third copy is still useful, but a fifth or sixth is usually safe to recycle.

Rare and higher pets should almost never be recycled. Their income rate is high, their trading value is significant, and their collection bonuses are disproportionately valuable. The only legitimate reason to recycle a rare is to free up space for an even rarer pet, like trading a duplicate rare for room to accept a legendary. Even in that case, the trade should be made deliberately, not reactively, because losing a rare is a setback that takes a long time to recover from.

### An Organizational System

Experienced Build a Base and Steal players use an organizational system to manage their storage, even though the game does not provide folders or tags. The most common approach is the family-first system, where pets are mentally organized by family rather than by rarity. Each family is a mental bucket, and the player tracks how many pets they have in each bucket at all times. When storage gets tight, the player recycles the least useful pets from the most complete families first, because those pets are contributing the least to collection bonuses.

A second approach is the rarity-first system, where pets are organized by rarity tier and the player recycles the lowest-rarity pets first. This system is simpler but less efficient, because it ignores the family bonus structure that gives Build a Base and Steal its long-term depth. A common pet that completes a family bonus is more valuable than a duplicate rare that is not part of any active bonus, and the rarity-first system would recycle the common while keeping the duplicate rare, which is the wrong call.

A third approach is the income-first system, where pets are organized by their effective income rate (rarity times level times family bonus), and the player recycles the lowest-income pets first. This system is the most rigorous but also the most labor-intensive, because it requires constant recalculation as collection bonuses change. Most players use a hybrid of family-first and income-first, prioritizing family completion for the active family they are working on, and recycling the lowest-value pets from completed families.

## When to Expand Storage

The decision of when to expand storage is one of the most common questions new players ask. The general rule is to expand before you need to, not after. If you find yourself unable to roll because storage is full, you have waited too long. The right time to expand is when you have the cash, the expansion will free you up to roll for a specific pet you are chasing, and the cost is small relative to your weekly income.

A practical test: if your storage is at 80 percent capacity and you are planning a major rolling session, expand first. The cost of one expansion is much smaller than the cost of the rolls you would lose to auto-recycle, and the storage headroom lets you roll aggressively without worrying about overflow. Conversely, if your storage is at 50 percent and you have no immediate rolling plans, hold off on the expansion. Storage expansion is a tool, not a status symbol, and it should be used when it improves your actual gameplay, not when you feel like you should have more space.

## The Long-Term Storage Mindset

The final piece of pet storage management is the long-term mindset. As your collection grows into the hundreds, the question shifts from "do I have room for this pet" to "does this pet earn its slot." Every slot in your storage has an opportunity cost: the cash that slot could be generating by holding a different pet, or the cash you could be earning by rolling for a new pet. A disciplined long-term player keeps their storage lean, recycles aggressively, and treats storage expansion as a strategic investment rather than a default upgrade.

The leanest veteran players keep their storage at 60 to 70 percent of capacity, which gives them room to roll for new pets without constant expansion, and they recycle the lowest-value pets every few sessions to keep the collection healthy. The players who fill their storage to the cap and then expand again and again usually end up with bloated collections that are not optimized for the current meta, and they spend more time managing their inventory than they spend playing the game.
