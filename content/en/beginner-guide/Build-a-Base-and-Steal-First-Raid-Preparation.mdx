Your first raid in Build a Base and Steal is a milestone moment, and how you approach it determines whether raiding becomes a reliable income source for you or a frustrating expense that sets you back. Most beginners either avoid raiding entirely because they are afraid of failure, or they jump in unprepared and lose their first few attempts, which shakes their confidence. The right path is a deliberate preparation process that maximizes your chance of success on the very first try, and this guide walks you through that process from target selection all the way through to the extraction.

## Why Your First Raid Matters

The first raid is not about the loot. It is about proving to yourself that you understand the raid mechanics well enough to execute a successful attack. A successful first raid, even a small one, builds confidence and starts the positive feedback loop that turns raiding into a regular activity. A failed first raid, especially if it was avoidable, can discourage a player from raiding for weeks, which means they miss out on one of the most important income sources in the game.

The second reason the first raid matters is that it sets your habits. Players who prepare carefully for their first raid tend to prepare carefully for their second, third, and tenth raids. Players who wing it on the first raid tend to wing it on subsequent raids, and the inconsistency shows up in their success rate. The cost of preparation is small (an extra 10 to 15 minutes before the raid), and the payoff is large (higher success rate, better loot, faster progression).

### Step 1: Choose a Beginner-Friendly Target

The first step in any raid is choosing a target, and the first raid should target a base that is significantly weaker than your own. The strength of a base can be estimated from the outside: walls, traps, and visible pet count. A base with one wall layer, no visible traps, and three to five visible pets is a beginner-friendly target. A base with multiple wall layers, visible traps, and a dozen or more visible pets is not.

Beyond the visual estimate, the raid interface also shows some metadata about the target, like its rough defense level and its rough loot value. The defense level is a numeric estimate that combines wall tier, trap count, and base size. The loot value is a numeric estimate of the cash and pets in the base. For your first raid, look for a target with low defense level (under 3) and moderate loot value (between 1,000 and 5,000 cash equivalent). These targets are designed to be raided by new players, and the success rate is high.

Avoid the temptation to raid a high-value target on your first attempt, even if the loot would be transformative. The probability of success is low, the cost of failure is high (you will lose the gear you used), and the psychological impact of a failed first raid is disproportionately negative. There will be plenty of high-value targets to raid in the future, once you have the experience and the gear to handle them.

### Step 2: Prepare the Right Gear

Once you have chosen a target, the next step is to prepare your gear. The gear you need depends on the target's defenses, but for a beginner-friendly target, the basic loadout is sufficient. The basic loadout includes one weapon (the basic blaster or whatever starter weapon you have), one tool (a basic breaching charge), one support item (a basic shield), and one consumable (a basic damage boost).

The gear you bring should be appropriate for the target. Bringing your best gear to a beginner target is wasteful, because you will use up the durability on a raid that did not require it. Bringing your worst gear is risky, because a failure could have been avoided with slightly better tools. The right call is to bring gear that is one tier above the minimum required for the target, which gives you a margin of safety without wasting your best equipment.

Check your gear before you launch the raid. The weapon should be fully charged or have full durability. The tool should be unexpired (some consumables expire after a certain time). The support item should be ready to use. The consumable should be unused. Launching a raid with depleted gear is a common cause of failure, and it is easily avoided by a 30-second check.

### Step 3: Approach the Target Base

When you launch the raid, you spawn at the entrance to the target base. Take a moment to survey the base from the spawn point before you move. Note the location of the walls, the entrance, any visible traps, and the location of the pets if they are visible. The 10 to 15 seconds you spend observing at the spawn are worth far more than the time you save by rushing in, because they let you plan your approach instead of reacting to obstacles.

The approach should be cautious but not slow. Move at a moderate pace, watch for traps (which usually have a visible trigger zone on the ground), and use cover when available. Most beginner-friendly targets do not have complex trap layouts, but they may have a single trap near the entrance to deter casual raids. Watch the ground in front of you as you move, because trap trigger zones are visible as a slight color change or texture difference.

If the base has a single entrance, that is the only path to the loot. If the base has multiple entrances, you can choose the one that looks least defended. Multiple entrances usually mean the defender split their defensive budget across more areas, so each entrance is weaker than a single entrance on a smaller base. Take the weakest entrance and push through quickly.

### Step 4: Breach and Engage

Once you are at the first wall, use your breaching charge to open a path. A basic breaching charge destroys one wall segment in a single use, so a base with one wall layer requires one charge. A base with two wall layers requires two charges, which is why beginner-friendly targets usually have only one wall layer.

After the breach, move through the opening and engage any defenses. Beginner-friendly targets may not have active defenses, but some have automated turrets or pet-based guards. These can be dispatched with your weapon, but use cover and your shield to minimize the damage you take. The shield is most valuable in the first few seconds of engagement, when the turret or guard is most accurate.

If you encounter a trap inside the base, slow down and look for the trigger. Traps are visible if you know what to look for: a slightly different floor texture, a small object on the ground, or a brief glow when you are close. The trigger is usually a tile or a small radius around an object. Step around the trigger, and the trap will not activate. If you cannot avoid it, take the damage and keep moving, because standing still in a trap is worse than moving through it.

## Step 5: Extract Your Loot

Once you reach the pets, the extraction is the final step. Loot extraction works automatically when you touch a pet, but the time it takes depends on the pet's rarity. Common pets extract in 2 seconds. Uncommon pets take 4 seconds. Rare pets take 8 seconds. Legendary pets take 15 seconds. The longer extraction times for higher-rarity pets are a built-in risk, because a defender or a counter-raider can interrupt the extraction.

Beginners should focus on extracting commons and uncommons first, because they are fast and the cumulative value is meaningful. If you have time after collecting the low-rarity pets, move to the next highest value. If the raid timer is running low (you usually have 60 to 90 seconds for a basic raid), extract what you have and exit through the same path you entered.

Do not try to extract a legendary on your first raid, even if one is visible. The extraction time is long, the risk of interruption is high, and the failure would set you back significantly. There will be other legendaries in other raids. Your first raid is about building the habit of successful completion, not about hitting a home run.

## Step 6: After the Raid

After a successful raid, take a moment to review what worked and what did not. The loot you extracted is your reward, but the lesson you learned is more valuable. If the raid went smoothly, you have validated your target selection and your gear choice. If the raid was harder than expected, you have learned something about the target's actual difficulty that will inform your future choices.

The post-raid review should be quick. Note the target's actual defense level (which the raid interface shows you after completion), the time it took, and any close calls you had. This data feeds into your next target selection, and over time it builds a mental model of which bases are easy, which are hard, and which are not worth the cost. Players who review their raids consistently improve faster than players who just collect the loot and move on.
